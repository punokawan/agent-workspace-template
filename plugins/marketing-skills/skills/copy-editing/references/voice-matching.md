# Voice Matching

How to read a brand's voice from a sample and apply it to an edit or a rewrite. Use it after the AI tells are out (see Humanizing AI Drafts in SKILL.md), or whenever an edit has to sound like an existing author.

## Get a Sample

Start from the product marketing context ([where it lives](../../product-marketing/SKILL.md#where-the-context-lives)) if it has a voice section or writing examples. If it doesn't, ask for one piece the user considers on-brand. One concrete sample tells you more than adjectives like "friendly but professional."

## Build the Voice Profile

Answer these from the sample and write the profile down before editing:

| Question | How to read it |
|---|---|
| Sentence length | Count the words in 10 sentences. Around 15 or fewer reads punchy; 25 or more reads flowing |
| Formality | Contractions ("it's" vs. "it is"), first person, slang, jargon left unexplained |
| Humor | Dry, self-deprecating, provocative, or none |
| Stance toward the reader | Peer (working it out together), expert (teaching), or challenger (you're probably doing this wrong) |
| Signature habits | Phrases, constructions, or punctuation that repeat |
| What's absent | Words, topics, or devices the brand never uses |

Match the profile, not the sample's topic. When the sample uses a device the AI-tells reference caps (fragments, say), keep it at the frequency the sample uses it and no higher.

## Voice Stances

Most brand voices sit near one of these four. Use them to name the target, then apply it through the profile.

| Stance | Profile | Where it fits | How to apply |
|---|---|---|---|
| Direct expert | Short sentences, confident claims, few qualifiers | Developer tools, no-fluff B2B, opinionated founders | Lead each section with the conclusion; state claims without softening; keep paragraphs to 1-2 sentences |
| Thoughtful peer | Longer sentences when the thought needs them; shows the reasoning | Community-led B2B, newsletters, founder blogs | Show how you reached the conclusion; say what you don't know; tell the mistake before the lesson |
| Provocateur | Takes positions most brands avoid, with evidence behind them | Contrarian analysts, challenger brands | Open with the claim you'll defend; answer the strongest counterargument; state the uncomfortable number and let it stand |
| Enthusiastic practitioner | Energy for the craft and its details | Creative tools, marketing tech, practitioner communities | Share the detail only a practitioner would notice; use the field's terms without over-explaining |

A stance changes emphasis and register. It never licenses the patterns in the AI-tells reference: a provocateur still states the claim directly, without a staged "everyone says X" setup.

## Real Material Only

Two devices make copy sound lived-in, and both depend on facts the user supplies:

- **The anecdote**: what the team did, what happened that they didn't expect, and what it taught them. "We rewrote the onboarding copy first. Activation moved only after we cut the load time." Use stories the user gives you; otherwise mark `[NEED: founder story]`.
- **The honest admission**: what the team got wrong, doesn't know, or tried and dropped. Readers trust it because brands rarely say it. Write it only from what the user confirms.

Never invent experience, customers, quotes, or numbers to make a voice feel real.

## Voice Check

After the edit:
- [ ] Read it aloud. Does it sound like a person you'd follow?
- [ ] Can you tell the paragraphs apart, or do they all share one rhythm and length?
- [ ] Is there at least one moment where this brand's personality is unmistakable?
- [ ] Would someone who knows the brand's other content recognize it as theirs?
- [ ] Does it say at least one thing most brands in the category wouldn't?
